04About

My story

I have always been more interested in the connections than the individual pieces.

Long before I worked with AI or planetary data, I was fascinated by patterns, systems, and understanding how seemingly separate observations fit together.

That curiosity led me to studying physics because I wanted a broad foundation for understanding the universe. I pursued a bachelor’s degree in the field, then midway through my bachelor’s, I studied at the University of Padua with a focus on astrophysics, then graduated at the top of my class. Physics taught me that much of what we know about the universe cannot be observed directly. We understand it by combining signals, measurements, models, and different perspectives.

That idea eventually led me to AI.

Today, as a PhD candidate at The Ohio State University, I work across artificial intelligence, computer vision, remote sensing, and planetary science. My research explores how AI can interpret complex planetary data, reason across multiple sources of information, and support scientific analysis, exploration, and mission planning.

I also teach a geodesy and mapping course for about a hundred students, where the basics of measuring a world are the same ones my research depends on.

What may look like different fields to others has always sparked the same motivation in me: finding the right connections between them.

I’m interested in a simple question with long consequences: how do we, and the machines we build, come to understand something we can’t see all at once?

01Research and publications

From planetary data to scientific reasoning and mission planning.

My research follows a broader goal: building AI systems that can interpret complex multimodal planetary data, perform scientific reasoning across multiple sources, and reduce the time and manual effort required for scientific analysis, exploration, and mission planning.

  1. 01

    LUCID: LUnar Caption Image Dataset

    LUCID is a large-scale lunar vision-language dataset built from real NASA mission observations. It pairs 96,000 high-resolution lunar images with scientifically grounded captions and includes approximately 81,000 visual question-answer pairs.

    The dataset uses complementary information from LROC imagery, GRAIL gravity measurements, and LOLA-derived terrain slope to connect visible surface features with broader geological context.

    Data + scientific context

    ✦ 96K image–caption pairs and 81K question–answer turns, released openly

  2. 02

    LLaVA-LE: Large Language-and-Vision Assistant for Lunar Exploration

    LLaVA-LE, introduced in my CVPR 2026 AI4Space paper, uses LUCID to adapt a vision-language model specifically for lunar exploration.

    Through concept alignment and instruction tuning, the model learns to describe lunar terrain, answer scientific questions, and reason about surface and geological characteristics from real lunar observations.

    Vision + language + scientific reasoning

    ✦ 3.3× better than the base LLaVA model overall

  3. 03

    LUMINA: LUnar Mission INspection Agent

    LUMINA is an agentic AI system for lunar landing-site assessment and mission planning, presented at DPS 58.

    Rather than producing a single model response, LUMINA works toward a mission objective by coordinating lunar datasets, deterministic geospatial tools, mission constraints, measurements, and evidence. It can screen and rank candidate regions, assess terrain, calculate routes, and produce traceable evidence-backed reports.

    Agents + tools + mission objectives

Where they come together

Together, these projects represent an evolution in my research: LUCID provides the scientific data. LLaVA-LE investigates how AI can interpret and reason about that data. LUMINA explores how AI agents can coordinate tools, evidence, and mission requirements to support larger planetary exploration tasks.

Dataset

LUCID

LUnar Caption Image Dataset

The first real, multimodal lunar dataset for vision-language models: panchromatic images from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera, co-registered with gravity and slope maps, each paired with a detailed scientific caption.

96Kimage–caption pairs
81Kquestion–answer turns
3co-registered modalities
28 × 28 kmper 224 × 224 px patch, at 125 m per pixel

An example from LUCID

How it was built

LROC tiles of 1504 × 832 pixels at 125 m per pixel were each split into 60 patches of 224 × 224 pixels, about 28 × 28 km each. Captions were written by a language model with a structured prompt, using the co-registered gravity anomaly and slope maps as reference, and each caption was turned into 3 to 5 question–answer pairs.

What the questions cover

Formation and evolution, general description, geomorphology, spatial relations and surface properties.

How it compares

The largest real dataset of its kind, and the only one that brings imagery, gravity and slope together.

Model

LLaVA-LE

Large Language-and-Vision Assistant for Lunar Exploration

A vision-language model trained on LUCID to interpret lunar observations: it describes what an orbital image shows and answers scientific questions about it.

LLaVA-LE interprets lunar observations; the LUMINA framework develops multistep landing-site assessment.

3.3×overall gain over the base LLaVA model
2.1×gain over training on captions alone
1.07reasoning score, above the judges’ own reference answers (1.0)
Role
Lead author
Authors
Gokce Inal, Pouyan Navard, Alper Yilmaz
Published
CVPR 2026 Workshops (AI4Space), CVF Open Access · arXiv 2603.24696

Examples

Problem

General vision-language models have seen very little planetary data. They describe a lunar image the way they would describe a photograph: fluent, but generic, and sometimes invented.

Approach

LLaVA-LE starts from LLaVA-v1.5-13B with a CLIP ViT-L/14 vision encoder. The encoder and language model stay frozen; only the projection layer and LoRA modules are trained, in two stages: 76K image–caption pairs to align lunar visuals with geological language, then 81K question–answer pairs to teach conversation and reasoning.

Evaluation

190 questions on 50 held-out lunar patches, scored by GPT and Gemini judges for relevance, clarity and accuracy. Scores are normalised by each judge’s own reference answer, so 1.0 matches the reference.

Agent

LUMINA

LUnar Mission INspection Agent

An AI agent that plans, inspects orbital maps, checks its conclusions against physics and explains why a site is, or is not, a good place to land.

LLaVA-LE interprets lunar observations; the LUMINA framework develops multistep landing-site assessment.

Lead researcher; presented in an oral talk and press briefing at DPS 58
Authors

Published
AAS Division for Planetary Sciences, 58th meeting, 2026 (abstract)
Problem

Choosing where to land on the Moon means weighing slope, hazards, illumination, communication with Earth and what lies beneath, across data that were never designed to be read together. Today that synthesis is slow, manual expert work.

Why it matters

Missions to the lunar south pole and beyond need many candidate sites assessed quickly and transparently. A system that reasons across layers, and shows its reasoning, can help experts focus their attention where it matters.

Approach

LUMINA treats assessment as multi-step reasoning: it plans which evidence it needs, inspects the relevant maps, checks whether they agree with known physical relationships, and then assesses the site.

Data

Orbital maps of the 85–90°S polar cap from LROC QuickMap: terrain and slope, long-term sunlight, the view of Earth and permanently shadowed regions.

System

Built on LLaVA-LE, the vision-language model that reads the lunar imagery.

Results

All seven of NASA’s candidate landing regions in the study area appear among LUMINA’s top 16 proposals, found without seeing NASA’s list. Preliminary results were presented at DPS 58 in 2026; full evaluation, baselines and ablations are in progress.

Bio

Gökçe Alina İnal earned her bachelor’s degree in Physics from Akdeniz University, graduating with honors and first in her department. During her undergraduate studies she conducted research on the characterization of radiation detectors, and spent part of her studies at the University of Padova (Italy), focusing on astrophysics. She is currently pursuing a PhD in machine learning at The Ohio State University’s Photogrammetric Computer Vision (PCV) Lab.

Her research brings together computer vision, remote sensing, and planetary science. Using satellite data from NASA’s lunar missions (LROC, GRAIL, LOLA), she works on lunar surface analysis, landing-site selection, and autonomous mission planning. She is the developer of LLaVA-LE, the first open-source lunar vision-language model trained on real NASA mission data. Presented at CVPR 2026, it laid the groundwork for her agentic AI system for landing-site assessment and mission planning, which she recently briefed the press on at the American Astronomical Society’s Division for Planetary Sciences (DPS) meeting.

Alongside her research, İnal teaches a geodesy and mapping class at The Ohio State University.
